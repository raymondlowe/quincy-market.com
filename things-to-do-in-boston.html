Things to Do in Boston — Free, Indoor & Downtown Ideas

Last updated: October 2026. Hours, prices and seasonal schedules change — always confirm with the attraction before you travel.

Boston is one of the easiest big cities in America to see on foot, and many of the things people come for are free. The Freedom Trail links 16 official historic sites on a 2.5-mile brick line from Boston Common to Charlestown, and it costs nothing to walk. Boston Common has been public ground since 1634. Quincy Market, the 1826 food hall at the center of it all, is free to enter — you pay only for what you eat.

This guide answers the three questions visitors ask most: what is genuinely free in Boston, what to do indoors when it rains, and what to do downtown or around Boston on a day trip. It is written from Quincy Market, which sits on the Freedom Trail between the waterfront and the North End — a natural place to start the day and an easy lunch stop in the middle of it. For prices and dishes inside the food hall, see our Complete Food Guide with Price List.

Boston at a glance: Freedom Trail free to walk — 16 sites, 2.5 miles · Quincy Market and Faneuil Hall Marketplace entry free · Boston Common (1634) and the Public Garden (1837) free · USS Constitution and Bunker Hill Monument free · subway fare $2.40, 1-day pass $11.00 · food-hall meals $8–18 per person · 3 MBTA T stops within one block of Quincy Market.

Free things to do in Boston

Boston keeps its best-known sights free, which is unusual for a major US city with this much history. Everything in the table below can be done without buying a ticket — the only exceptions are the Partner sites on the Freedom Trail (the Old State House, Old South Meeting House, Paul Revere House and Old North Church), which are privately operated and charge admission.

Free attraction What it is Where
Walk the Freedom Trail 16 official historic sites over a 2.5-mile route from Boston Common to Charlestown, marked by a red brick line in the pavement. Guided tours with costumed guides are ticketed and book up, but the trail itself is free. Downtown Boston to Charlestown
Boston Common & Public Garden America's oldest public park (established 1634) and, next to it, America's first public botanical garden (1837) with the Lagoon and the Swan Boats. Boston Common, downtown
Quincy Market & Faneuil Hall Marketplace Free to enter — no ticket, no entry fee. A food hall with dozens of stalls and eateries, plus more than 40 specialty carts and shops in the marketplace. Downtown Boston, by Haymarket
Faneuil Hall and the Great Hall The hall that gave the marketplace its name — "the Cradle of Liberty", where America's first Town Meeting was held. The National Park Service runs free ranger talks in the Great Hall on the second floor when it is not in use for events, and the first-floor visitor center is free to walk into. Next to Quincy Market, downtown
Massachusetts State House Charles Bulfinch's 1798 capitol for the Commonwealth of Massachusetts — second stop on the Freedom Trail. Free guided tours run on weekdays (10:00–15:30) and advance booking is requested; the building is closed at weekends. Freedom Trail, stop 2 (by Boston Common)
Old State House & Boston Massacre site The outside is free to see: the 1713 Old State House is the oldest surviving public building in Boston, and the cobbled circle in front marks the Boston Massacre of 5 March 1770. Going inside is a ticketed Partner site. Downtown Boston, Freedom Trail
Boston Public Library, Central Library Free to all, and genuinely museum-quality: the McKim Building opened in 1895, with a marble courtyard, murals, gallery spaces and free Art & Architecture tours. A good rainy-day stop with no ticket attached. 700 Boylston St, Copley Square
Rose Kennedy Greenway A free linear park through downtown — gardens, fountains, public art and a seasonal carousel — running from Chinatown past the waterfront to the North End. Downtown Boston
USS Constitution Launched in 1797, the oldest commissioned warship still afloat and free to board. Visitors 18 and over must show photo ID and pass a security screening. The adjoining museum is free with suggested donation. Charlestown Navy Yard
Bunker Hill Monument Marks the battle of 17 June 1775 — the first major battle of the Revolutionary War. Free to visit; there is no entrance fee at the federally owned sites of Boston National Historical Park. Charlestown

Two smaller freebies worth knowing: the Frog Pond on Boston Common is a public rink from November through March (skating is run in partnership with The Skating Club of Boston, with skate rental and concessions on site) and a spray pool in summer, open daily 11:00–18:00, alongside a carousel and café — check bostonfrogpond.com for seasonal hours and fees. And the HarborWalk lets you follow the waterfront on foot — a way to see the harbor without booking a cruise.

Indoor things to do in Boston — and what to do on a rainy day

Boston weather turns fast. The good news is that the two things visitors most want to see — the market hall and the historic sites — are already indoors, and they are free. Here is the rainy-day order we recommend.

Indoor option Cost Notes
Quincy Market food hall Free entry The obvious rainy-day lunch: one covered building, dozens of stalls, $8–18 per person. Note that the food hall closes earlier than the rest of the marketplace on Sundays (typically 12:00–18:00).
Faneuil Hall: Great Hall + NPS visitor center Free Shelter, restrooms and free ranger talks on the second floor when the hall is not booked for an event.
Boston Public Market Free entry Indoor, year-round market at 100 Hanover Street, directly above Haymarket T station, with more than 30 New England producers and prepared food. Open daily: Sun–Tue 8:00–18:00, Wed–Sat 8:00–20:00.
Boston Public Library, Central Library Free Architecture, art and a covered courtyard to sit out a downpour, right by Copley station.
Museum of Science Adult $33 · child (3–11) $28 · senior $29 · under 3 free Open daily 9:00–17:00 — the classic whole-afternoon rainy-day answer for families.
New England Aquarium Adult $39.95 · child (3–11) $30.95 · under 2 free · senior $37.95 At 1 Central Wharf, a short walk from Quincy Market. Fall hours are weekdays 9:00–17:00 and weekends 9:00–18:00; weekends and holidays often sell out, so book ahead.
Massachusetts State House Free Weekdays only, and indoors throughout — free tours run from mid-morning to mid-afternoon.

If the rain settles in for the afternoon, pair the market with the museum: Quincy Market for lunch, then the Aquarium on the waterfront, then a walk back under cover through the marketplace. If you have children and a full wet day, our pick is the Museum of Science.

Things to do in downtown Boston

Downtown Boston is compact — you can cover the historic core in a half day without using the subway once. Everything below is inside a walkable loop around Quincy Market.

The Freedom Trail's downtown stretch

Start at Boston Common (established 1634 — America's oldest public park) and follow the brick line east: the Massachusetts State House (1798), Park Street Church, the Granary Burying Ground, King's Chapel, the Boston Latin School site and the Old Corner Bookstore all sit within a few minutes of each other. Then comes the Old State House (1713), the Boston Massacre site (5 March 1770), Faneuil Hall, Quincy Market and the North End. Children will recognize the geography long before they recognize the history.

Quincy Market and Faneuil Hall

This is the downtown lunch stop. Quincy Market is the historic 1826 food hall — the building itself, not the whole complex; if you are unsure which is which, our Quincy Market vs Faneuil Hall explainer settles it in one line. Faneuil Hall is the older meeting hall in front of it, and the National Park Service visitor center inside is free to walk into — rangers there hand out maps and trail information.

The waterfront and the Greenway

The Greenway is a free ribbon of gardens, fountains and public art that threads through downtown past the waterfront; the Aquarium itself is on Central Wharf. Walk back through the Haymarket area and you pass Boston Public Market, which shares a block with the T station of the same name.

Downtown shopping and Boston's old streets

The Faneuil Hall Marketplace itself has more than 40 specialty carts and shops, which makes souvenir-hunting part of the same walk. Beyond downtown, Back Bay has the bigger retail addresses — Newbury Street, and the connected Prudential Center and Copley Place — all a short T ride from the Common.

Quincy Market and Boston's food halls — the market angle

If you only do one food thing in Boston, do the food hall. Quincy Market is free to enter, covered, central and cheap by city standards: a substantial item from a counter stall runs about $8–15, and a sit-down meal in one of the pubs or restaurants around the perimeter about $10–18 per person. The market trades on New England classics — clam chowder in a bread bowl, lobster rolls, Boston cream pie, baked beans and brown bread — alongside pizza, poke bowls, Greek, Mexican, Japanese and dessert stalls.

Three practical notes. First, the lunch crush is 11:00–14:00; arrive before 11:00 or after 14:00 and the queues shorten dramatically. Second, on Sundays the food hall closes earlier than the wider marketplace, so plan an early lunch rather than a late one. Third, the market is a natural halfway stop on a Freedom Trail walk, so you can eat here and keep going rather than doubling back.

Things to do around Boston — day trips by train

"Around Boston" is one of the easiest wins in the region: the T and the Commuter Rail reach most of the classic day trips without a car. These are the four we would pick, in order of how little effort they take.

Day trip Why go Getting there
Cambridge Harvard (founded 28 October 1636, the first college in the American colonies) and MIT, plus Harvard Square's bookshops and cafés. Red Line to Harvard or Kendall/MIT
Salem The waterfront town north of Boston and the region's biggest October draw — its Haunted Happenings season dominates the month. Commuter Rail, Newburyport/Rockport Line from North Station — $8.00 one way, about 30 minutes; a $10 weekend pass covers unlimited weekend travel
Lexington & Concord Minute Man National Historical Park preserves the ground of the opening engagements of the Revolutionary War, on the road between the two towns. Commuter Rail, Fitchburg Line from North Station (Concord and Lincoln stops), then a walk or a local bus
Boston Harbor Islands 34 islands and peninsulas inside the harbor — lighthouses, tide pools, trails and views back at the skyline. Seasonal ferry from Long Wharf North (Blue Line: Aquarium), roughly a 35–45 minute crossing to Georges or Spectacle Island

Salem deserves the warning it always gets: October (its "Haunted Happenings" season) brings extra weekend trains and heavy crowds, and parking in Salem is restricted around Halloween. Go by rail, and go early. For the islands, the ferry season runs from spring into October and tickets are best booked in advance — and note that the ferry itself costs money even though the islands are free to visit.

What a day in Boston actually costs

Boston can be done expensively or cheaply, and the difference is mostly about how many ticketed attractions you add. Here are the numbers you will meet on this page.

A frugal day — walk the Freedom Trail, browse Quincy Market, cross to Charlestown for the USS Constitution — costs little more than a T fare and lunch, roughly $10–18 plus transport. Children aged 11 and under ride the T free, which helps families more than any coupon.

Boston in one day — an itinerary that works

This route uses the Freedom Trail as its spine and starts at Quincy Market, so you can arrive, eat and walk without backtracking.

Time What to do
09:00 Boston Common and the Public Garden — the 1634 park and the 1837 botanical garden, then pick up the red brick line.
10:00 State House (1798), Park Street Church, Granary Burying Ground, King's Chapel and the Old Corner Bookstore — all within a few blocks.
11:00 Old State House (1713) and the Boston Massacre site (1770), then on to Faneuil Hall.
11:45 Quincy Market: free ranger talk in the Great Hall if one is running, then lunch in the food hall ($8–18).
13:30 North End: Paul Revere House (about 1680) and the Old North Church, where two lanterns were hung on 18 April 1775.
15:00 Cross to Charlestown for the USS Constitution (1797) and Bunker Hill Monument (1775) — both free.
17:00 Back downtown along the Rose Kennedy Greenway and the waterfront, or into the Aquarium if you have booked tickets.
19:00 The marketplace and downtown are quietest after 19:00 — a good time for a second pass through Quincy Market or dinner on the periphery.

If the weather turns, run the rainy-day version instead: Boston Public Library and the Museum of Science or the Aquarium in place of the long walk, keeping Quincy Market as the lunch and dry-out point.

Getting to Boston and getting around

By air — Logan International Airport

A free Massport shuttle links the terminals with the MBTA Airport station on the Blue Line and the Rental Car Center; from Airport station the Blue Line runs downtown. The Silver Line 1 also runs directly from all terminals to South Station, and that journey from the airport into downtown is free. For the full step-by-step route to the market, see How to Get Here.

By train — Amtrak and Commuter Rail

South Station is the northern terminus of Amtrak's Northeast Corridor, with direct services to and from New York, Philadelphia and Washington, D.C.; Amtrak also stops at Back Bay and North Station. Commuter Rail day trips (Salem, Concord, Rockport and others) leave from North Station, which is also an MBTA subway hub.

In the city — the T

The subway is called "the T" (MBTA). A one-way subway fare is $2.40 with a CharlieCard, CharlieTicket, contactless card or cash; local bus is $1.70; a 1-day pass is $11.00 and a 7-day pass $22.50. Children aged 11 and under ride free. Since 1 September 2026 the MBTA has been piloting unlimited transfers between subway, bus and ferry within a two-hour window, provided you pay with the same method throughout — useful if you are hopping between downtown, the waterfront and Back Bay.

Three T stops are within a block of Quincy Market: Haymarket (Green Line, about 2 minutes' walk), Government Center (Green, about 4 minutes) and Aquarium (Blue, about 3 minutes), with State Street (Orange) on the same corner as Haymarket. Driving is the expensive option — downtown parking is best pre-booked, and the parking guide lists the official garage and the alternatives.

Hours, contact details and the market's own opening times are collected on the Useful Information page.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best free things to do in Boston?

Walk the Freedom Trail (16 sites, 2.5 miles), spend time in Boston Common and the Public Garden, walk into Quincy Market and the Faneuil Hall Marketplace, see the Great Hall and the free National Park Service ranger talks at Faneuil Hall, visit the Boston Public Library's Central Library in Copley Square, and cross to Charlestown for the USS Constitution and Bunker Hill Monument — all free.

What is there to do in Boston when it rains?

Start with the market: Quincy Market and the Faneuil Hall Marketplace are covered and free to enter, and Boston Public Market at 100 Hanover Street is an indoor, year-round food market above Haymarket station. For a full wet afternoon, the Museum of Science (adult $33) and the New England Aquarium (adult $39.95) are the two big indoor options, and the Boston Public Library is free.

What should I do in downtown Boston?

The downtown core is walkable in half a day: Boston Common, the Massachusetts State House, the Old State House and the Boston Massacre site, Faneuil Hall and Quincy Market, then the Rose Kennedy Greenway and the waterfront. Most of it is free.

What can I do around Boston without a car?

Plenty. The Red Line reaches Cambridge (Harvard and MIT); the Commuter Rail from North Station reaches Salem in about 30 minutes for $8.00 one way, and continues towards Concord and Rockport. In season, ferries from Long Wharf North run to the Boston Harbor Islands.

Is Quincy Market worth visiting if I am only in Boston for a day?

Yes — it is free, central, on the Freedom Trail, and a full meal in the food hall costs about $8–18. It also works as the plan B for rain, because the food hall is indoors. Budget $8–18 per person and aim to eat before 11:00 or after 14:00 to avoid the crush. Full prices and dishes are in the Food Guide.

In short: Boston's headline attractions are free — the Freedom Trail, Boston Common, the Public Garden, Quincy Market and Faneuil Hall, the USS Constitution and Bunker Hill Monument. Add the Boston Public Library and the Rose Kennedy Greenway for indoor and rainy-day backup, and use the T ($2.40 a ride, $11.00 a day) to reach Cambridge, Salem or the Harbor Islands. Start and finish at Quincy Market: free entry, food for $8–18 a person, three T stops within a block and the Freedom Trail running right past the door.
